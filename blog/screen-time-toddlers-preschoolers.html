Screen Time for Toddlers and Preschoolers: What the Guidelines Say

Clear, age-by-age guidance on screens, and gentle ways to make more room for play, talk and books at home.

Photo by Anil Sharma on Unsplash

November 16, 2026

Mobile phones, the TV and tablets are part of almost every home today. A cartoon during dinner or a video while you finish cooking can feel like a small lifesaver on a busy day. Yet many parents in Aurangabad ask our teachers the same question: how much screen time is really okay for a little child? The good news is that clear guidance exists, both from the World Health Organization and from India's own paediatricians. Here is what it says, and how to put it into practice without turning every evening into a battle.

What the Guidelines Say, Age by Age

In 2019 the World Health Organization published guidelines for children under five. The Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) followed with its own detailed guidelines on screen time and digital wellness. Their advice for young children is very similar:

  • Under 2 years: no screen time. The IAP makes one exception: short, occasional video calls with family members who live far away, like a chat with grandparents in another city.
  • 2 to 5 years: no more than 1 hour a day in total, and the less the better. The IAP suggests keeping each sitting to about 20–30 minutes.

That one hour includes everything: TV, cartoons on the phone, tablet games and videos playing in the background.

Why Less Screen Time Matters in the Early Years

The years from two to six are a time of huge growth. Children learn to talk by hearing real voices and getting answers. They build strong hands and bodies by running, climbing, stacking and scribbling. They learn to wait, share and manage big feelings through everyday interactions with the people around them.

A screen cannot talk back in the way a parent, grandparent or older sibling can. Time spent watching is time not spent playing, chatting or sleeping. The IAP also recommends that children aged two to five get at least three hours of physical activity across the day and 10–14 hours of sleep, and screens can easily eat into both.

Simple Family Rules That Work

Clear, predictable rules are easier for little ones than a new negotiation every day. The IAP suggests a few that fit Indian homes well:

  • No screens at mealtimes. Many children have learnt to eat only while watching videos. Try sitting together at the dining table or on the floor, talking about the day and naming the foods on the plate.
  • No screens in the hour before bedtime. Replace the phone with a bath, a story and a cuddle.
  • Screen-free zones. Keep the bedroom, the dining area and the car or two-wheeler ride free of gadgets for everyone in the family.
  • One screen at a time. Switch off the TV if nobody is watching it.

When You Do Use Screens, Make Them Count

Screens are not the enemy, and an occasional programme is perfectly fine for a preschooler. A few habits make that time more useful:

  • Choose slow, gentle, age-appropriate content, ideally in the language your family speaks at home, whether that is Marathi, Hindi or English.
  • Watch together when you can. Ask simple questions: "Where do you think the elephant is going?" or "How many birds can you see?"
  • Avoid using the phone as the only way to calm a crying or bored child. It works in the moment, but little ones soon learn to expect it every time.
  • Use a timer, and give a five-minute warning before screen time ends so the switch-off feels less sudden.

Be the Role Model

Children copy what they see far more than what they are told. If we scroll through our phones at the dinner table, they will want to do the same. The IAP encourages families to keep a daily "digital fasting" time when nobody uses a device. Even 30 minutes after dinner, with phones kept in another room, can become a lovely family ritual of stories, songs or a short walk around the colony.

Easy Screen-Free Ideas for Indian Homes

The hardest part is often knowing what to offer instead. A few simple ideas using things you already have:

  • Let your child help in the kitchen: washing vegetables, rolling a small roti or sorting rajma and chana.
  • Build a "house" with a bedsheet and two chairs, then play pretend inside it.
  • Sing rhymes and action songs in your home language.
  • Read a picture book together, or make up a story about your child's day.
  • Head out in the cool winter evenings of Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar for a walk in the park or a game of catch.

For many more ideas, see our post on fun home activities for toddlers. And if switching off the TV leads to tears, our guide to helping your child navigate emotions has gentle ways to handle those moments.

Small Steps, Big Difference

You do not have to change everything overnight. Start with one rule, perhaps no phone at dinner, and add more once it feels normal.
