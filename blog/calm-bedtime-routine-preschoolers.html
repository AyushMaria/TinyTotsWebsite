Building a Calm Bedtime Routine for Preschoolers

A few simple steps, done the same way every night, can turn bedtime battles into a calm, cosy end to the day.

Photo by Vitaly Gariev on Unsplash

November 23, 2026

Many parents in Aurangabad tell our teachers that the hardest part of the day is not the morning rush but bedtime. One more story, one more glass of water, one more cartoon, and suddenly it is past ten o'clock and everyone is tired and cross. A calm, predictable bedtime routine can change that. It helps little ones wind down, fall asleep more easily and wake up ready for a happy day at home or at preschool. Here is how to build one that works for a busy Indian family.

How Much Sleep Do Little Ones Need?

According to the World Health Organization, children aged 1 to 2 need 11 to 14 hours of good quality sleep, including naps, and children aged 3 to 4 need 10 to 13 hours, which may include a nap. The WHO also recommends regular sleep and wake-up times.

In many of our homes, dinner is late and evenings are lively, with grandparents, cousins and neighbours dropping in. That is part of the joy of family life. But if your child must be up by 7 for school, working backwards from that wake-up time will tell you roughly when lights should be off.

Why a Routine Works So Well

Young children feel safe when they know what is coming next. When the same few steps happen in the same order every night, the body and mind begin to recognise them as signals that sleep is near. The routine itself becomes the cue, so there is less need for coaxing, bargaining or scolding.

The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that children thrive on a regular bedtime routine and suggests a simple pattern like "brush, book, bed". It also advises keeping the same waking time on weekdays and weekends, as far as possible.

A Simple 30-Minute Wind-Down

Your routine does not need to be long or complicated. Twenty to thirty minutes is plenty for most preschoolers. Here is one example you can adapt:

  1. Quiet signal: dim the lights and lower the noise in the house. A soft "bedtime is coming" song can mark the start.
  2. Wash and change: a warm wash or bath, then comfortable night clothes. In the cool winter nights of Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar, a light sweater or a soft blanket may be welcome.
  3. Brush teeth: let your child have a turn first, then help finish the job.
  4. Toilet: one last visit so there are fewer wake-ups in the night.
  5. Story and cuddle: one or two picture books, a story from your own childhood, or a lullaby in Marathi or Hindi.
  6. Lights out: a hug, a short goodnight phrase you always use, and then leave the room calmly.

Keep the order the same every night. A small picture chart on the wall showing each step can help children who like to know "what comes next".

Keep Screens Away Before Sleep

Cartoons and mobile games are exciting, and they can leave little minds too busy to settle. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends turning off all screens at least one hour before bedtime and keeping screens out of children's bedrooms, especially at night. Our earlier post on screen time for toddlers and preschoolers has more ideas for gentle screen rules at home.

In place of the phone, try a few calm activities in that last hour: a simple puzzle, colouring, threading big beads, or helping to fold small clothes from the laundry.

Make the Room Sleep-Friendly

  • Keep the room as dark and quiet as your home allows. A thick curtain helps with street lights.
  • Make sure your child is comfortable, neither too warm nor too cold.
  • Offer a favourite soft toy or a small cotton dupatta that smells of home.
  • Keep the bed for sleeping and cuddles, not for active games.

Handling Stalling, Tears and Night Wake-Ups

Almost every preschooler tests bedtime now and then. A few gentle ideas from our teachers:

  • Offer small choices: "Do you want the elephant story or the moon story?" Choices give a sense of control without changing the plan.
  • Plan for the usual requests: keep a little water nearby and do the toilet visit before lights out, so "one more thing" has already been taken care of.
  • Stay calm and boring: if your child gets up, walk them back to bed with few words and a quick hug.
  • Acknowledge feelings: some children feel worried at night. Naming the feeling, "You feel a little scared in the dark", helps more than "There is nothing to be scared of". Our post on helping your child navigate big emotions has more ideas.

Getting the Whole Family On Board

In joint families, bedtime can be shared between parents, grandparents and older siblings. That is wonderful, as long as everyone follows roughly the same steps. A quick chat about the routine helps, so that Aaji, Ajoba or Nani know that the last hour is for quiet stories, not exciting games or sweets.

Weekends, weddings and festivals will sometimes push bedtime late, and that is perfectly fine. Simply return to the usual routine the next night.

Small Steps Towards Better Nights

Start small: pick a fixed bedtime and two or three steps, and keep them steady for a couple of weeks before adding more. Most children settle into a new rhythm sooner than parents expect. If your child snores loudly, often wakes in distress, or seems very tired during the day despite a good routine, please speak to your paediatrician.
